September 1966.
A fishing boat is heading home across the South Pacific,
a long way from anywhere.
The man at the wheel is an Australian named Peter Warner.
He is looking through his binoculars at an island
that nobody lives on.
It is a steep green rock called Ata,
and it has been empty for a hundred and three years.
But there are burnt patches on the cliffs.
And in the tropics,
green does not catch fire by itself.
Then a boy dives off the rocks and swims out to the boat.
He tells Warner there are six of them.
They have been here for fifteen months.
Warner gets on the radio to the Tongan capital.
The answer that comes back stops him cold.
Those boys had been given up for dead.
Their funerals had already been held.
Twelve years earlier,
a British schoolteacher had published a novel
about what happens to boys with no adults on an island.
They turn on each other.
They hunt.
They kill.
You may have been made to read it in school.
It is called Lord of the Flies.
These six boys were all alive,
and all healthy.
And a big part of the reason goes back to a crime
on that same island,
one that most people telling this story never mention.
This is No Rescue.
True stories of people who survived when no one
was coming.
If you read Lord of the Flies in school,
or if you grew up in Tonga or anywhere in the Pacific,
tell us in the comments:
did anyone ever tell you this happened?
Nuku'alofa,
the capital of Tonga,
June 1965.
St Andrew's was a boarding school there,
and the six came from it.
Their ages depend on who you ask.
A report from the time says sixteen to eighteen.
A recent book says thirteen to sixteen.
One of the boys,
Mano,
says he was nineteen.
Another,
David,
says he was fifteen.
Their names were Luke Veikoso,
Stephen Latu,
Sione Fataua,
David Siola'a,
Kolo Fekitoa and Mano Totau.
They do not agree on why they left,
either.
David has said that they sold their rugby boots,
and were afraid of being punished.
Mano has said that they were bored.
Another account makes it a simple pleasure trip.
What they agree on is the boat.
It was a sailing boat,
twenty-four feet long,
and it belonged to a man named Taniela Uhila.
They did not ask him.
They sailed north,
and dropped anchor about six miles out.
Then a storm hit them.
It broke the anchor.
It tore the sail.
It snapped the rudder.
They rigged up a blanket to catch what wind they could,
and after that they went wherever the sea took them.
There was nothing to steer with,
and nothing to eat.
They drifted for eight days.
By one estimate,
they covered about two hundred miles.
Nobody knew where they were.
Then there was a shape on the horizon.
Ata is the top of an old volcano,
standing alone,
a hundred miles south of the main island of Tonga.
Its cliffs go straight down into the water.
They swam for the rocks.
Mano says he got there first,
at night.
Nobody lived there.
Nobody had lived there for a hundred and two years.
They lived in a cave,
in the beginning.
They ate what the island gave them,
which was seabirds.
They ate the meat raw,
took the eggs,
and drank the blood.
They caught fish,
and they ate those raw too.
They found rainwater standing in the hollows of
old trunks.
This is the point in the story where,
according to the novel,
things are supposed to go wrong.
Six hungry boys,
no adults,
and a place where no one will ever know what you did.
But something else happened first.
They went exploring.
About three months in,
they climbed up onto the plateau,
the high ground above the cliffs.
And there they found the ruins of a village.
There was a water tank cut into the rock,
and an old machete.
And there was food.
Taro,
growing wild.
Bananas.
Breadfruit.
And chickens,
running loose,
the descendants of birds that somebody had once kept.
The boys had gone from eating raw seabirds to
having a farm.
They built pens for the chickens.
They laid out a garden.
Most people who tell this story mention that farm
in one line,
as a bit of good luck,
and they keep going.
But the question is sitting right there.
Who planted it?
And where did they go?
We will get to that.
First,
there was one thing they still did not have.
For three months,
they had eaten everything raw.
Stephen was the one who kept trying,
with two sticks,
rubbing one against the other,
until the day it worked.
They could not be sure they would ever manage it again.
So they did not let it go out.
According to the historian Rutger Bregman,
who interviewed two of them,
that fire burned for more than a year.
And here is what they did with the time.
They split up into pairs,
with a job for each pair:
one in the garden,
one in the kitchen,
and one on watch for ships.
They made a place to exercise.
They even played badminton.
Kolo built a guitar out of driftwood,
with six wires salvaged from the wreck of the boat,
and they sang the same few songs for over a year.
They tried to build a raft.
The accounts differ on how that ended,
but it did not get them off.
Nobody was hunted.
Nobody was killed.
You may have read more than that.
Popular versions of this story have a broken leg
set with sticks,
a doctor amazed at the result,
and a rule for settling every fight.
We went looking for those.
We could not confirm them to our standard,
so we are leaving them out.
What is left is strange enough.
Because now we can go back to the ruins.
A hundred and two years earlier,
Ata was not empty.
Around three hundred people lived there,
on the plateau,
in a village called Kolomaile.
They grew bananas and sugarcane,
and sold them to passing ships.
In June 1863,
a brig called the Grecian anchored off the island.
Her captain was a whaler named Thomas McGrath.
A few weeks earlier,
according to a newspaper report at the time,
McGrath had proposed a new line of business to his crew:
the slave trade.
The people of Ata came on board to trade.
McGrath invited them to eat first.
Below decks,
the ship's cook had prepared a feast.
They went down.
And the crew locked the hatches.
At least one hundred and forty-four men,
women and children were taken from Ata that day.
That was close to half of the island.
The details of the meal are a reconstruction,
by the historian Scott Hamilton.
The ship,
the captain and the number come from what the
newspapers printed that same year.
McGrath sailed on to another Tongan island,
Niuafo'ou,
and tricked thirty more young men.
Then he sold them all to a Peruvian slave ship,
the General Prim.
A hundred and seventy-four people.
This was a trade with a whole history.
In the space of about two years,
ships took roughly three thousand people from the
Pacific islands to Peru.
The General Prim reached the port of Callao that July.
By then,
under pressure from abroad,
Peru had already cancelled the licenses.
So nobody wanted them.
They were kept in a warehouse by the harbor,
and smallpox broke out.
Peru ordered the traders to take the islanders home.
They did not take them home.
They took more than four hundred islanders,
from across the Pacific,
and left them on an island off the coast of Costa Rica.
When a Peruvian naval ship finally came,
thirty-eight were still alive.
It carried them to the port of Paita.
And there,
the record ends.
A letter mentions a man from Ata,
met at a horse race in 1894,
after years in South America.
Nobody has been able to verify it.
Not one of them is known to have come home.
Back on Ata,
about half the village was left.
Word reached the king of Tonga,
George Tupou the First.
He sent ships and took them all off the island,
to Eua,
a larger island to the north.
Nobody has lived on Ata since.
They left behind their houses and their water tank.
They left their bananas,
their taro,
and their chickens.
Some of those first refugees are buried on the
west coast of Eua.
For generations,
it was not a good thing,
on Eua,
to be from Ata.
One family was blamed for the raid,
on the strength of rumor.
As for McGrath,
he was arrested at the port of Bluff in New Zealand,
on charges that had nothing to do with slavery.
There is no evidence he was ever tried for what
he did at Ata.
He appears to have gone back to whaling,
and he died in Tahiti.
A hundred and two years went by.
The island's food went on growing,
with no one to eat it.
Then six boys climbed up from the rocks,
hungry.
The people of Ata,
half of them stolen and the rest taken away,
had planted the food that fed them.
September eleventh,
1966.
Peter Warner was the son of a prominent Melbourne man,
Sir Arthur Warner,
and a sailor good enough to win the Sydney to
Hobart race three times.
He was out with a crew of four,
on a boat called the Just David.
He passed close to Ata,
and saw those burnt patches in the green.
Then he saw figures on the rocks,
and one of them went into the water.
It was Stephen,
the boy who had made the fire.
He swam out to the boat.
Warner radioed Nuku'alofa,
and that was when he heard about the funerals.
Their families had already mourned them.
Then the boat came in,
with all six of them alive.
You would think that was the end of it.
But Taniela Uhila had not forgotten his boat.
The boys were locked up for stealing it.
Fifteen months on a rock,
and they ended up in a cell.
It was Warner who got them out.
He paid the owner a hundred and fifty pounds.
He got the money by selling the rights to the
story to Channel Seven in Sydney.
The accounts differ on the details.
But the film exists.
It is called The Castaways,
it came out that October,
and one copy survives.
So the first time that the world saw this story,
it belonged to an Australian television station.
Warner did not walk away from them,
and that part should be said plainly.
He hired them on his boats.
He got a lobster concession from the king,
and from 1968 he lived in Tonga.
In 1974,
when a wrecked yacht called the Sospan Fach was found,
it was Mano who saw the signals.
Warner died in April 2021,
at the age of ninety,
at sea,
crossing the bar at Ballina in Australia.
But a year before that,
in May 2020,
this story went everywhere.
Rutger Bregman told it in a book and in The Guardian.
Millions of people read it as the real Lord of the Flies,
as proof that people are good.
Soon a Hollywood studio,
New Regency,
had bought the rights,
for a sum reported in the low seven figures.
And that was when Tongan and Pacific voices
started to speak up.
The writer Meleika Gesa-Fatafehi argued that the
story was being told through the man who found them,
that its colonial history was missing,
and that its rights were in foreign hands.
Above all,
she argued that you cannot explain what those
boys did without Tongan values of community.
Tahlea Aualiitia raised objections of her own.
Taika Waititi called for Polynesian filmmakers to make it.
Bregman answered that he had interviewed Mano and
Sione himself,
and that his book does cover the slave trade.
And the survivors have their own view.
Mano has said he has no problem with Warner
holding the rights.
You can decide for yourself who gets to own a
story like this one.
But it does change the novel,
doesn't it?
William Golding imagined English schoolboys,
and what they would do once civilization was taken away.
Nobody took anything away from these six.
They brought it with them.
So what do you make of a story like this?
Nobody was coming for them.
There had been funerals.
If a man with binoculars had taken a different line home,
there is no telling when anyone would have known.
For fifteen months,
they looked after themselves,
and they did it together.
But they did not do it alone,
and that is what stays with me.
Every banana they ate up there came from a
village that was lied to,
locked in a hold,
sold,
and scattered.
People whose names we mostly do not have.
Whose story was left out when the world fell in
love with this one.
They never knew it.
But a hundred and two years later,
they fed six boys.
Their descendants still live on Eua,
and the historian who followed their trail says
something has started to change.
People there have begun to feel proud of being from Ata.
Six boys came through it together,
with a fire,
a guitar and a garden.
Now think about how the same thing looks with
nobody at all.
A man alone,
at the bottom of the sea,
in the dark,
with no one to take over the watch.
His story is on your screen now.
If this story stayed with you,
subscribe to No Rescue.
Every week,
we tell another true story of someone who
survived when no one was coming.
Thank you for watching.
