STAY WITH THE AIRCRAFT
MODERN SURVIVAL RULE
1958.
A survey plane from a British oil company is
crossing the Libyan desert,
hundreds of miles from the sea.
Below it,
on a flat plain of gravel,
lies an American bomber.
Nobody had reported a plane missing out here.
The nearest American air base had no record of one.
When a ground team finally reached it,
they found it broken behind the wings,
but almost whole.
One of its machine guns still fired.
All anyone had to do was pull the trigger.
Its radio was pulled out and fitted into another plane.
It worked perfectly.
There was food on board.
There was water.
There were no parachutes.
And there was nobody inside.
The nine men who flew her had jumped in the dark,
about twelve miles away.
At first light,
the eight who found each other made a choice.
They started walking.
Away from the plane.
This is not a story about men who did something stupid.
It is a story about three decisions.
Each one was reasonable.
Each one was made with the information they had.
And about the one piece of information they never got.
This is No Rescue.
True stories of people who survived,
or tried to,
when no one was coming.
If you are a pilot,
a navigator,
or you ever served in an air crew,
tell us in the comments:
have you ever trusted an instrument that was
telling you the wrong thing?
Soluch,
Libya.
The fourth of April,
1943.
A dirt airstrip near Benghazi,
on the coast of North Africa,
home to the bombers of the 376th Bomb Group.
That day,
the group was sending its B-24 Liberators north,
across the Mediterranean,
on a high altitude raid against the port of Naples.
One of them carried the serial number 41-24301.
Her crew called her Lady Be Good.
For all nine men on board,
it was their first combat mission.
The pilot was First Lieutenant William Hatton.
His copilot was Robert Toner.
The navigator was a second lieutenant named Dp Hays.
With them flew John Woravka,
Harold Ripslinger,
Robert LaMotte,
Guy Shelley,
Vernon Moore and Samuel Adams.
Nine men,
from New York,
Massachusetts,
Missouri,
Ohio,
Michigan,
Pennsylvania and Illinois.
The weather was against them from the start.
Blowing sand swept the airfield,
and many of the bombers had to turn back.
Lady Be Good took off after noon,
one of the last to leave.
Thirty minutes short of Naples,
she turned around.
The records do not settle why.
Poor visibility,
perhaps.
Or sand in the engines.
Now she was heading home.
Alone,
at night,
above a layer of cloud.
To understand everything that happens next,
you need to understand how a crew found its way
home in 1943.
Over open sea at night,
there is nothing to see.
No coast,
no roads,
no lights.
A navigator works out where he is from speed,
heading and time.
Dead reckoning.
And he can check it by radio.
A station on the ground listens to your
transmission and tells you the bearing it hears you on.
That night,
the bearing came from a station at Benina,
near Benghazi.
But the radio loops of the time had a weakness.
They could tell you the line you were on.
They could not always tell you which end of the
line you were at.
A bearing of three hundred and thirty degrees,
and a bearing of one hundred and fifty,
sit on the same line.
Pointing in opposite directions.
Just after midnight,
Hatton radioed that his automatic direction
finder was not working properly,
and asked for the location of his base.
The help he asked for never reached him.
The bearing the crew had to work with was three
hundred and thirty degrees.
North-west of Benina.
Still out over the sea.
Seventeen years later,
the Army investigators wrote down what they
believed happened.
The navigator,
they concluded,
had probably taken a reciprocal reading.
The right line,
pointing the wrong way.
Lady Be Good was not north-west of her base.
She was south-east of it.
She had already flown past.
So the first decision was not really a decision at all.
It was a number,
and a crew that trusted it.
They kept flying.
Strong tail winds may have pushed them along even
faster than they thought.
Somewhere in the dark below the clouds,
they crossed the coast and passed their own airfield.
Nobody on board knew.
In the words of the official report,
the pilot flew into the desert thinking he was
still over the Mediterranean.
And from the air,
at night,
a flat desert and a calm sea look exactly the same.
Black,
and endless.
Lady Be Good flew on.
Four hundred and forty miles.
Into the Sahara.
By two in the morning,
the fuel was almost gone.
Think about what the men in that cockpit believed.
They were over the sea.
They were lost.
They could not find their base.
Putting a heavy bomber down on the water at night
is one of the most dangerous things a crew can attempt.
The B-24 had a bad reputation for breaking apart
when it hit the sea.
So the pilot made the call that a careful pilot
would make.
Everybody out.
That was the second decision.
And with what they knew,
it was the right one.
All nine men jumped into the dark.
They believed they were jumping into the sea.
Instead,
they hit sand.
Robert Toner carried a small diary.
Later he wrote down that night in a few blunt words.
Got lost returning.
Out of gas.
Jumped.
Landed in the desert at two in the morning.
Somehow,
in the dark,
they found each other.
Toner's next words are a roll call.
No one badly hurt.
Can't find John.
All others present.
John was John Woravka,
the bombardier.
His parachute had not opened properly.
He would not be found for seventeen years.
Eight men were standing in the Libyan desert.
And above them,
with no one on board,
the plane flew on.
Three of her four engines had stopped.
On the last one,
Lady Be Good came down on her belly on the gravel
and slid for nearly seven hundred yards.
She broke behind the wings.
But she held together.
In her fuselage were water,
food,
and a radio that still worked.
The men had no idea where she was.
They had no reason to think she had survived at all.
The sun came up on the fifth of April.
Now picture what they could see.
Flat gravel in every direction.
No hills,
no trees,
no road.
Nothing.
And picture what they knew.
They had flown a long way over the sea.
They had been heading home.
Home was on the coast.
They did not know they had flown over land.
As far as they knew,
the coast had to be close.
And the coast was to the north.
So the third decision almost made itself.
Walk north.
Reach the coast.
Find the base,
a road,
a patrol.
Anyone.
Before you hear what happened,
stop for a moment and put yourself there.
Eight men.
Between all of you,
half a canteen of water.
You believe the sea is a short walk away.
Do you sit and wait to be found?
Or do you walk?
And here is the thing about waiting.
Nobody knows where you are.
Your base thinks you went down at sea.
If anyone is searching,
they are searching the water.
And they were.
The search went out over the Mediterranean.
They were the only crew that did not come back
from that mission,
and nobody thought to look inland.
Write down what you would have done.
Then keep watching.
They walked north.
What they could not know was where they really were.
Not a few miles from the coast.
Hundreds of miles inland.
About twelve miles away,
in a direction they had no reason to look,
sat a plane with water and a working radio.
And ahead of them,
to the north,
lay the Calanscio Sand Sea.
They set out with half a canteen between eight men.
They expected to be followed.
So as they went,
they left a trail.
Arrows cut from parachute cloth,
laid flat on the ground and weighed down with stones.
A pair of fleece-lined flight boots,
set down with the toes forming an arrow pointing north.
Signs for the rescuers who would surely come.
On this part of the desert,
the temperature at midday could reach one hundred
and thirty degrees Fahrenheit.
Fifty-four Celsius.
And the nights are cold.
Toner kept writing as they walked.
His entries are short.
They tell of the walk north,
and of how much it cost them.
Years later,
before anyone knew how far they had gone,
desert survival experts were asked to estimate it.
Twenty-five miles on foot,
they said.
Thirty at the very most.
Keep that number in mind.
At first the ground was hard and flat,
and they made good distance.
Then came the sand sea.
Sand that gives way under every step.
Dunes,
one after another,
some of them six hundred feet high.
Five of them could go no further.
Hatton,
Toner,
Hays,
LaMotte and Adams stopped together,
on a rise inside the sand sea,
to rest and to wait.
By then they had walked about eighty-five miles.
The other three kept going.
Guy Shelley,
Harold Ripslinger and Vernon Moore went on north
into the dunes,
to find help and bring it back for the others.
Toner's diary covers eight days.
From the fifth of April to the twelfth.
Then the entries stop.
The three who went ahead pushed on further than
anyone thought possible.
Guy Shelley made it another twenty-one miles.
Harold Ripslinger went on another twenty-six
miles beyond that,
climbing the side of a great dune.
Vernon Moore has never been found.
Remember what the experts said.
Thirty miles at the very most.
Following their trail,
Ripslinger had covered something like one hundred
and thirty miles.
On half a canteen of water.
Nobody came.
Back at Soluch,
Lady Be Good was listed as missing.
Everyone assumed what the crew had assumed.
That she had gone down somewhere in the Mediterranean.
The war moved on.
For fifteen years,
the desert kept her.
Then in 1958,
geologists flying for the D'Arcy Oil Company
spotted the wreck from the air.
They reported it to Wheelus Air Base,
near Tripoli.
The base had no record of an American plane lost
in that part of the desert.
In March 1959,
a ground team drove out to the site.
A surveyor named Gordon Bowerman wrote to a
friend at Wheelus,
the base commander.
He listed what he had found.
Maintenance records.
Names written on clothing and equipment.
Those names led back to April 1943.
And the Army's mortuary service in Frankfurt took
up the case.
That summer,
an Army captain named Myron Fuller and an
anthropologist named Wesley Neep searched the
desert from May to August.
Nineteen miles north of the plane,
beside the tracks of an old wartime convoy,
they found the flight boots.
Still pointing north.
Then the parachute arrows.
One after another.
And then the sand sea,
where the trail disappeared.
Their equipment broke down.
The dunes kept shifting.
The search was called off.
They found no one.
In February 1960,
a team from British Petroleum was working deep
inside the sand sea.
On a rise in the dunes,
they came across five men lying close together.
Hatton,
Toner,
Hays,
LaMotte and Adams.
Around them were canteens,
flashlights,
pieces of parachute,
flight jackets.
And Robert Toner's diary.
That diary is how we know most of what you have
heard tonight.
The Army and the Air Force mounted one last search.
It was called Operation Climax.
Nineteen men,
six vehicles,
two helicopters,
and reconnaissance jets photographing the dunes
from high above.
On the twelfth of May,
an oil company crew found Guy Shelley.
Five days later,
a helicopter crew spotted Harold Ripslinger,
on the slope of a high dune.
In August 1960,
another oil team found John Woravka,
still in his flying suit,
his parachute still attached.
About twelve miles from the plane.
Less than half a mile from him,
the Air Force team found the parachute harnesses
the other eight had left behind on the night they jumped.
The place where it all began.
By the end,
the searchers had covered more than six thousand
square miles.
Vernon Moore is still out there.
Among the things the Army brought home was a
wristwatch belonging to one of the crew.
It was still running.
It kept time to within ten seconds a day.
And part of a survival kit,
with eight squares of caramel still inside.
In January 1961,
the airmen at Wheelus unveiled a stained glass
window in their chapel,
for the crew of Lady Be Good.
Today it hangs in the National Museum of the
United States Air Force,
in Ohio.
It is easy to judge this story from where we sit.
The navigator,
Dp Hays,
has been criticized for decades.
And for more than sixty years,
people have asked the obvious question.
Why did they walk away from the plane?
Modern survival training gives downed air crews a
simple rule.
If you can,
stay with the aircraft.
A plane is much easier to spot from the air than a person.
But look again at what these men actually knew.
They did not walk away from the plane.
As far as they knew,
there was no plane to go back to.
They had no idea where it had come down,
or whether anything was left of it.
They did not choose the desert over the sea.
They thought the sea was a short walk ahead.
They did not ignore their instruments.
They trusted them.
Given the world they believed they were in,
every decision they made after that bearing was
the right one.
Keep flying,
because home is ahead.
Jump,
because you cannot put a bomber down on the sea at night.
Walk north,
because nobody is searching the sand,
and the coast is close.
The fatal error was made hours earlier,
in the dark.
A number that could mean two opposite things,
read the only way that made sense to them.
Everything after that was eight men doing their
best with the wrong map in their heads.
And what they did with it was extraordinary.
The experts said thirty miles.
They walked more than eighty,
as a group.
They kept marking a trail for rescuers who did
not know they existed.
And three of them went on alone,
into the dunes,
for the ones they left behind.
So here is the question this story leaves you with.
Not what you would have done if you had known everything.
What you would have done at dawn on the fifth of April,
with only what they knew.
Tell us in the comments.
This was No Rescue.
