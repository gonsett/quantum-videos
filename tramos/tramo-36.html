NEXT ON THE LAST QUESTION
One day,
there will be a last sunrise.
Not just on Earth.
Everywhere.
Somewhere in the universe,
a small red star will burn through the very last
of its fuel.
It will flicker,
and cool,
and fade.
And when it does,
no new star will ever take its place.
After that,
the universe will keep going for longer than you
can possibly imagine.
Long enough for every black hole to boil away
into nothing.
Long enough,
perhaps,
for atoms themselves to fall apart.
This is not a myth,
and it is not a prophecy.
It is what our best physics predicts,
written down in equations,
and tested against the sky.
Tonight,
we are going to travel to the end of time.
Era by era,
from the death of our own Sun,
to the very last flash of light the cosmos will
ever produce.
And at the end of that journey,
there is a question.
A question a writer asked in 1956,
that physicists are still arguing about today.
Can it all begin again?
This is The Last Question.
The biggest questions ever asked.
If you have ever looked up at the night sky and
wondered how it all ends,
subscribe.
And stay with me,
until the last light goes out.
In November 1956,
a small science fiction magazine published a
story by Isaac Asimov.
It was called,
The Last Question.
It begins in the year 2061,
with two technicians,
slightly drunk,
standing next to a giant computer.
They are celebrating.
Humanity has just learned to draw its energy
directly from the Sun.
Enough energy,
they think,
to last forever.
But one of them is not so sure.
The Sun will not last forever,
he says.
Nothing will.
So,
half as a joke,
they ask the computer a question.
Can the decline of the universe be reversed?
The machine goes quiet.
It thinks.
And then it answers.
Insufficient data for meaningful answer.
The story then jumps forward.
Thousands of years.
Millions.
Billions.
Trillions.
Each time,
humanity has changed beyond recognition.
And each time,
someone asks the very same question.
Asimov later said it was his favourite of all the
stories he ever wrote.
Because the question in that story is not fiction.
It is one of the deepest problems in physics.
And it was first asked,
seriously,
a century before Asimov.
In 1852,
the physicist William Thomson,
who would later become Lord Kelvin,
published a short paper with a very dry title.
On a universal tendency in nature to the
dissipation of mechanical energy.
His conclusion was anything but dry.
Within a finite period of time to come,
the earth must again be unfit for the habitation
of man as at present constituted.
Thirteen years later,
the German physicist Rudolf Clausius gave the
idea its name.
Entropy.
Entropy is,
roughly speaking,
a measure of how spread out energy is.
Of how much disorder there is in a system.
And Clausius summed up its law in a single sentence.
The entropy of the universe tends to a maximum.
Every star that shines,
every engine that runs,
every heartbeat,
takes concentrated energy and spreads it out.
And it never goes the other way on its own.
Ice melts in a warm room.
A cup of coffee cools down.
It never heats itself back up.
And the universe,
slowly,
is cooling down too.
The end point of that process has a name that
sounds almost gentle.
The heat death.
Not a fire.
Not an explosion.
Just a universe where everything is the same temperature,
and nothing can ever happen again.
But how long does it take to get there?
What happens along the way?
And is that really the only way the story can end?
For most of the twentieth century,
nobody could say.
Then,
in 1997,
two young physicists at the University of
Michigan did something no one had quite done before.
Fred Adams and Greg Laughlin sat down and worked
out the entire future of the universe.
Step by step.
From now,
until the end.
Their paper was called,
A Dying Universe.
And it is the map we will follow tonight.
To read that map,
we first need a new kind of clock.
The universe is about thirteen point eight
billion years old.
That sounds like an unimaginably long time.
It is not.
Adams and Laughlin realised that ordinary numbers
are almost useless for the future.
So they counted time differently.
Not in years,
but in powers of ten.
They called these steps,
cosmological decades.
Ten years is one decade.
A hundred years is two.
A thousand years is three.
Each step on this clock is ten times longer than
the step before it.
Human history,
from the first cities to today,
fits inside decade four.
The dinosaurs lived and died within decade eight.
The Sun and the Earth were born in decade nine.
And everything that has ever happened,
the Big Bang,
the first stars,
the first cells,
every human being who has ever lived,
all of it fits inside the first ten decades.
The future,
as we will see,
runs to a hundred decades.
And possibly,
far beyond.
Here is another way to feel it.
Imagine squeezing the whole lifetime of the universe,
until its last black hole is gone,
into a single year.
On that calendar,
the entire history of the cosmos so far,
all thirteen point eight billion years,
would last less than the shortest moment physics
can even describe.
Less than a flicker.
Less than a heartbeat of a heartbeat.
In other words,
the universe has barely been born.
Almost everything that will ever happen has not
happened yet.
We are not living near the end of the story.
We are living in the first line of the first page.
So let us start the clock.
And let us start close to home.
Our Sun is about four and a half billion years old.
It is roughly halfway through its life as a normal star.
It shines by fusing hydrogen into helium deep in its core.
As the helium builds up,
the core slowly contracts,
and heats up.
And so the Sun gets brighter.
Very slowly.
About one percent every hundred million years.
That does not sound like much.
But the climate of the Earth sits on a knife edge.
In around a billion years,
the extra sunlight will push our planet past a
tipping point.
The oceans will begin to evaporate.
Water vapour traps heat,
which evaporates more water,
which traps more heat.
Long before the Sun dies,
the Earth will become a dry and scorching world.
A second Venus.
Life may hold on for a while,
deep underground,
or in the last shrinking pools.
But the blue planet,
as we know it,
has an expiry date.
Even the sky will change.
The Moon is slowly drifting away from us,
a few centimetres every year.
Today,
by a beautiful coincidence,
it looks almost exactly the same size as the Sun.
That is why we have total solar eclipses.
In several hundred million years,
the Moon will have drifted too far to cover the
Sun completely.
The last total eclipse will happen,
and no one may be there to see it.
Then,
about five billion years from now,
the Sun will run out of hydrogen in its core.
And it will do something dramatic.
Its core will contract and heat up.
And its outer layers will swell.
Out past Mercury.
Out past Venus.
The Sun will become a red giant.
Hundreds of times wider than it is today.
Whether it swallows the Earth is still debated.
As the Sun swells,
it blows away part of its own mass.
Its grip weakens,
and the planets drift outward.
In 2008,
two astronomers,
Klaus-Peter Schröder and Robert Connon Smith,
ran the numbers in detail.
Their conclusion.
The Earth probably does not escape.
The bloated Sun raises a tide on our planet,
and that tide drags the Earth inward,
until it spirals into the Sun's outer atmosphere.
And is gone.
Then the giant will exhale.
Its outer layers will drift away into space,
glowing for a few thousand years as a planetary nebula.
Like these.
They are some of the most beautiful objects in the sky.
And every one of them is the last breath of a
star like ours.
What remains at the centre is the Sun's naked core.
A white dwarf.
About half the mass of the Sun,
crushed into a ball the size of the Earth.
A single teaspoon of it would weigh several tonnes.
It will not burn anything.
It will simply glow,
with the heat it has left.
And it will spend the rest of eternity cooling down.
That is the fate of our Sun.
But by then,
the sky above the solar system will look
completely different.
On a clear autumn night,
far from city lights,
you can see a faint smudge in the sky.
It is the Andromeda Galaxy.
Hundreds of billions of stars,
two and a half million light years away.
It is the most distant thing most people will
ever see with their own eyes.
And it is falling towards us,
at about a hundred and ten kilometres every second.
In 2012,
astronomers used the Hubble Space Telescope to
measure Andromeda's sideways motion across the
sky for the first time.
The answer seemed clear.
A head-on collision.
In about four billion years,
our two galaxies would crash into each other.
NASA even released images of what our night sky
would look like,
as Andromeda grew to fill it.
For more than a decade,
that was the textbook story.
Then,
in 2025,
a team led by Till Sawala ran the numbers again.
With new data from the Gaia and Hubble space telescopes.
And this time,
with the other large galaxies in our
neighbourhood included.
They found that the collision is far from certain.
In only about half of their simulations do the
Milky Way and Andromeda merge within the next ten
billion years.
And the chance of a collision in the next five
billion is only about two percent.
It is a good reminder of what science really is.
Not a list of fixed answers,
but our best measurement,
today.
Open to the next one.
But in the very long run,
gravity is patient.
Our galaxy and Andromeda are bound to each other.
Over enough time,
they will become one.
Astronomers already have a name for it.
Milkomeda.
And even then,
almost no stars will collide.
The space between stars is so vast that two
galaxies can pass right through each other,
like two clouds of dust that never touch.
What changes is the shape.
Two graceful spirals become a single,
vast,
featureless ball of old stars.
And far beyond it,
something else will be happening.
Something much stranger.
In 1998,
two teams of astronomers were studying exploding
stars in distant galaxies.
They expected to find that the expansion of the
universe was slowing down,
held back by gravity.
Instead,
they found it was speeding up.
Something,
which we now call dark energy,
is pushing space apart.
Faster and faster.
The discovery won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2011.
And it has a consequence that is almost poetic.
As space expands faster and faster,
distant galaxies are carried away from us faster
than their light can cross the gap.
One by one,
they slip over the horizon.
And their light will never reach us again.
In 2007,
two physicists,
Lawrence Krauss and Robert Scherrer,
worked out what that means for the astronomers of
the far future.
Look at this image.
It is a tiny patch of sky,
smaller than a grain of sand held at arm's length.
And almost every point of light in it is a galaxy.
In about a hundred billion years,
every one of those galaxies will be gone.
Every galaxy beyond our own small group will have
slipped out of sight.
The night sky of Milkomeda will contain exactly
one galaxy.
Itself.
Surrounded by endless,
empty dark.
And the afterglow of the Big Bang,
the faint microwave light that fills the whole sky today,
will have been stretched and faded until it can
no longer be detected at all.
Now think about what that means.
An astronomer living then,
with telescopes far better than ours,
would look out and see one island of stars,
floating in a static,
unchanging void.
They would have no way of knowing that the
universe is expanding.
No way of knowing there was a Big Bang.
No trace of the hundreds of billions of other galaxies.
The evidence will not be hidden.
It will be gone.
Krauss and Scherrer put it in a single,
haunting line.
We are living at a very special time in the
evolution of the universe.
We live in a lucky moment.
Early enough to see the whole universe,
and to read its history in the light.
The astronomers of the future will not be so lucky.
They will reach the wrong conclusion.
And they will have no way to know it.
Now let us turn the clock forward.
Past ten billion years.
Past a hundred billion.
Past a trillion.
The great stars are long gone.
The brilliant blue giants that light up the arms
of galaxies live fast.
A few million years,
and then they explode.
Stars like our Sun last around ten billion years.
But the real survivors of the universe are smaller.
Much smaller.
Red dwarfs.

A kind of star that does not exist yet.
Anywhere in the cosmos.

Out of a hundred.
