## gancho
May 2013. Twenty miles off the coast of Nigeria, in the Gulf of Guinea.
A team of divers is working inside a ship that lies upside down on the seabed, a hundred feet below the surface.
They are not looking for survivors. Nobody is. The ship went over more than two days ago.
They have come to bring up the bodies. Four have already been recovered.
On the surface, the team watches the feed from a diver's helmet camera. Black water. A beam of light. Things drifting past.
Then a hand comes into the picture.
The diver reaches out to take it. And the hand grabs him back.
You may have seen that clip. Millions of people have. It lasts a few seconds, and it went around the world.
What the clip never tells you is how a man stays alive for sixty hours on the bottom of the sea, in the dark, in his underwear, in a pocket of air four feet high.
And the answer is stranger than the rescue.
This is the story of Harrison Okene, the ship's cook, and the air that should have killed him.

## the cook
To understand how he got into that pocket of air, you have to start in the place he came from.
Harrison Odjegba Okene was twenty-nine years old. He came from Warri, a city in the Niger Delta.
He was married. His wife, Akpovona, was twenty-seven.
The delta is one of the largest wetlands on Earth, a maze of creeks and mangrove where a great river breaks apart before it reaches the sea.
It is also the heart of the Nigerian oil industry. And a lot of that oil never touches a dock.
The biggest tankers cannot come in to the shallow coast. So they stop far out at sea and load from a buoy, with the oil coming through a line on the seabed.
Out there, the ocean does not hold still for anyone. A loading tanker has to be kept steady, and that is a job for tugboats.
Those boats stay out for long stretches. And the men on them have to eat.
That was Harrison Okene's job. He was not a diver. He was not a deck officer. He was the cook.

## the jascon
His ship was called the Jascon-4.
She was a tug built for heavy work offshore, pulling on loads far bigger than herself.
She belonged to West African Ventures, part of the Sea Trucks Group, and in May 2013 she was working on contract for Chevron.
The job was off Escravos, at the western edge of the delta. She was one of three tugs holding a tanker in place while it loaded.
The tug leans on its line and pulls, hour after hour, day and night, to keep the tanker from swinging on the swell.
There were twelve men on board.
It was routine work. And that week, the Atlantic swell was running heavy.

## ten to five
Sunday, the twenty-sixth of May, 2013. It was still dark.
By his own account, at around ten to five in the morning, Okene had got up and gone to the toilet. He was wearing only his boxer shorts.
Then the ship lurched.
What exactly happened in those seconds has never been fully explained in public. Reports speak of heavy swells, and of a sudden pull on the tow.
What is not in doubt is how fast it was.
The Jascon-4 rolled, and kept rolling.
The lights were gone. Everything he knew about the ship was suddenly in the wrong place.
He got out into the passage, in pitch darkness, with the deck turning under his feet.
He heard men shouting. He heard someone crying out for God to help him.
Three men were ahead of him, trying to get out.
And then the sea came in, at full force. It reached the three men first.
He never saw them alive again.
The water took him too. It carried him along the passage, through the inside of a ship that was turning over, and left him in a tiny washroom beside an officer's cabin.
Around him, the Jascon-4 was going down. She sank until she hit the seabed, and she settled there upside down, a hundred feet below the surface.
And when his head came up, there was air.

## four feet of air
Think of a glass pushed upside down into a sink. The water rises a little way inside it, and then it stops. The air has nowhere to go.
That is what had happened in one corner of the Jascon-4.
A pocket of air had been trapped against what was now the top of the room, and what had been the floor.
It was about four feet high. Below it, black water, and a ship full of open doors.
Everything was inverted. He held on to the washbasin, because it was the one thing that told him where he was.
He was almost naked, and he has described the water as freezing. Staying in it would finish him.
So he began to pull the panels off the wall, to make a tiny raft, enough to lift part of his body out of the water.
He had found two flashlights. Reports say he also had a bottle of Coca-Cola, and he took it in sips.
That was the whole inventory. No food. No fresh water. No way to tell anyone.
And a ship's hull is not a glass. There was no telling how long that air would stay.

## the dark
The flashlights lasted less than a day.
Then there was no light of any kind. The kind of dark where it makes no difference whether your eyes are open.
He could not tell night from day. He could not tell how long he had been there.
He said it plainly, later, to a reporter from Reuters.
I was there in the water in total darkness, just thinking it's the end.
The salt water took the skin off his tongue. He was hungry, but it was thirst that tormented him.
He kept hitting the hull with whatever he could find, hoping that someone up there might hear it. Nobody answered.
Since the first minutes, he had not heard another human voice.
There were other sounds in the water around him. He has spoken about them, and he has had to live with them. We will leave them there.
These were the men he had cooked for.
He has told that part in two ways. To one reporter, he said that he knew they were dead. Another was told that until his rescue, he believed they had escaped.
Perhaps both are true. In that dark, a man can know something and still refuse to believe it.

## the promise
So he did what people do. He prayed.
He went back over the verses he had been reading before he went to sleep.
Somewhere above him, past a hundred feet of water and twenty miles of sea, was the coast, and Warri, and Akpovona.
And down there, Harrison Okene made a promise to God.
If you rescue me, he said, I will never go back to the sea again. Never.
The hours kept going. Twelve. Twenty-four. Thirty-six.
He had no way of knowing it, but every breath he took was changing the air he had left.

## the surface
Up on the surface, nobody was counting those hours in his favor.
A tug had gone down. Twelve men had been aboard. Nobody had come up.
The search had turned, as it always does, into a recovery.
A dive team was sent in. The company was Dutch, DCN Diving, and its divers were South African.
These were saturation divers. It is one of the strangest jobs in the world.
They live for weeks in a sealed pressure chamber on the deck of a support ship, breathing a special gas mix, already squeezed to the pressure of the deep.
They ride to work in a steel diving bell, and they return to the chamber at the end of each shift.
It lets them work on the bottom for hours. But nothing about it is quick.
Going inside an overturned hull is about as dangerous as their work gets.
And they were doing it for the dead. Four bodies had been found. Nobody on that ship was expecting anything else.
One of the team, a life support technician called Alex Gibbs, put it in a single line.
Nobody thought there was anyone alive.

## the hammering
It was the twenty-eighth of May. He had been down there for almost sixty hours.
And then, through the steel, he heard it.
A hammering. Boom, boom, boom.
It was somewhere on the ship, and it was not the sea.
He knew what that sound meant. He hit the hull with everything he had.
Then he saw something he had not seen in two days. In the black water below him, there was a glow.
A diver's light, moving through the wreck.
And Harrison Okene did the most frightening thing he had done yet. He left his pocket of air.
He went down into the water, in total darkness, and he swam after that light.
It was gone before he could reach it.
In his own words: I tried to follow him in the pitch darkness, but I couldn't trace him, so I went back.
So I went back. Think about what that sentence means. Underwater, holding his breath, inside an upside-down ship, with no light at all, he had to find one small corner of air again.
He found it.
He had come within reach of the only people on Earth who could save him, and they had not seen him.
They were not looking for a man. They had no reason to wait.

## the hand
But the light came back.
A diver named Nico van Heerden was working through the compartments, and his helmet camera was sending every second of it to the surface.
What you are seeing here is our own reconstruction. The real footage is public, and it is easy to find.
This time, Okene went into the water and reached him. By his own account, he tapped the diver on the back of the neck.
Think about being that diver. You are alone inside a wreck, among the dead, and something touches you from behind.
He turned, and he saw a hand. And over his microphone, he said what anyone on that job would have said.
Corpse. Corpse. A corpse.
He reached out to take it. And Harrison Okene pulled on his hand.
Up above, the project manager, Tony Walker, was watching the screen.
It was frightening for everybody.
For a moment, nobody says anything that makes any sense. And then the voice comes over the line.
He's alive! He's alive!
Someone tells the diver what to do, and it may be the gentlest thing ever said on a dive radio.
Just reassure him. Pat him on the shoulder.
They got hot water to him, and they got him oxygen.
Harrison Okene had been alone in the dark for more than two and a half days. And now a stranger was patting him on the shoulder.

## the chamber
And here is the part that the clip leaves out completely.
He was found. But he could not leave.
A hundred feet down, the water presses on you with four times the pressure of the air at the surface.
He had been breathing that compressed air for sixty hours. All that time, the nitrogen in it had been soaking into his blood and his tissues.
If you go up quickly, that gas comes back out of solution, like the bubbles in a shaken bottle. It can block the vessels. It can kill you.
Petar Denoble, a researcher at the Divers Alert Network, did not soften it. Had he come straight up, he would have died.
So the only man who had survived the Jascon-4 had to be brought out very carefully, and very slowly.
They fitted him with a diving helmet, and they took him through the wreck he had lived in, to the bell.
At seven thirty-two in the evening, he reached the surface. Something like sixty-two hours after the ship went over.
That was not the end of it. He was moved into a decompression chamber, and the door was closed.
He spent about another sixty hours in there, in a steel tank, while the pressure came down a little at a time.
The man who had lasted sixty hours under the sea had to spend sixty more getting back from it.
That is why some headlines say he spent three days down there. It was closer to two and a half, and then two and a half again.
Ten bodies would be brought up from the ship. One man was never found.
Eleven men had died on the Jascon-4. He was the only one who came home.

## the air
When the video got out, the scientists started doing the arithmetic. And the arithmetic was not simple.
A resting human being needs a surprising amount of air. For sixty hours, one physicist worked out, roughly a thousand cubic feet.
His name is Maxim Umansky, at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory in California.
A thousand cubic feet is a small bedroom. Okene had a corner of a washroom.
But air at a hundred feet is squeezed to a quarter of its volume. There is four times as much of it in the same space.
So, in theory, a pocket about six feet on each side could have held enough oxygen. That part of the puzzle is not as hard as it looks.
Oxygen, though, is not what kills you in a sealed space.
What kills you is your own breath.
Every time you breathe out, you add carbon dioxide to the air. Let it climb to around five percent, and it begins to poison you. In a space that small, it should have got there long before the divers did.
So why did it not?
The answer was all around him. Carbon dioxide dissolves in water very easily. That is what makes a soda fizz.
All that time, the cold black water beneath him, the water that had trapped him, the water he was fighting to stay out of, was quietly pulling the poison out of his air.
The sea was cleaning what he breathed.
Nobody measured that pocket, so nobody can prove it. There may have been more air, connected, in another part of the wreck.
Eric Hexdall, a clinical director of diving medicine at Duke University, ran a separate estimate for National Geographic.
Symptoms at around fifty-six hours. Unconsciousness at around seventy-nine.
If that was right, the divers found him with less than a day to spare.
Asked how anyone could have lived through it, Hexdall did not reach for a formula.
I don't know what it was. It was divine providence.

## after
Harrison Okene had lived through something that no one could explain. And when he got home, he found out what that costs.
How does one man live, when eleven others die? People wanted an answer. His own pastor asked him whether he had used black magic.
He did not go to the funerals. He was afraid of what the families would say to him.
He would wake at night, sure that his bed was sinking.
And he kept his promise. He would not go near the sea. By the end of that year, he was cooking on dry land.
Then the whole world saw it.
In December 2013, about six months after the rescue, the helmet camera footage went online. News programs everywhere ran it. A hand in the dark. A voice saying, he's alive.
Millions of people watched the best moment of the worst days of his life, and then they scrolled on.
Nearly all of them saw only that hand. They did not see the three men in the passage, or the raft of wall panels, or the sixty hours in the tank.
And they did not see what happened next, which is the part that almost nobody believes.

## the diver
He had promised God that he would never go back to the sea.
He had meant it.
But in that wreck, a stranger had come down to him with a light. And according to reports from South Africa, he and Nico van Heerden had become friends.
In 2015, two years after the Jascon-4, Harrison Okene trained at a commercial diving school at Gordon's Bay, near Cape Town.
He got into the water. He put on the helmet. He went down.
The man who had spent sixty hours fighting to stay out of the water was learning to work in it.
He qualified. And those same reports say that the diploma was handed to him by the diver who had found him.
He kept going. He went on to earn a Class Two certificate as a commercial air diver, under the rules of the international offshore industry.
The cook of the Jascon-4 was going under the water for a living.
Ten years after the wreck, he said what it was to him now.
I'm enjoying diving. It's life for me. It's fun.

## no rescue
So what do you make of a story like this?
Nobody was coming for Harrison Okene. Every person on that ship was presumed dead, and the men who found him had come for the dead.
But think about why they came. They went down into a wreck, on the bottom of the ocean, to bring those men back to their families.
Because somebody thought that was worth the risk, one man went home alive.
He was not saved by a rescue. He was saved by a kind of respect.
And by a corner of air that should not have lasted, in a sea that kept cleaning it.
There is one more thing about those sixty hours.
While Harrison Okene lay in the dark off the coast of Nigeria, another man was alive on the far side of the world, in a small open boat on the Pacific.
He had already been adrift for six months. Nobody was looking for him either.
He had eight months still to go.
His story is on your screen now.
