## gancho
January 2014. A tiny island at the southern edge of the Marshall Islands, in the middle of the Pacific.
A couple who live there alone hear a man shouting on the beach.
He is almost naked. His hair and beard are long and wild. He holds a knife.
He does not speak their language. He is shouting in Spanish.
Behind him, on the sand, lies a small fibreglass boat, with a name painted on the bow.
That boat had left the coast of Mexico four hundred and thirty-eight days earlier.
Two men had been on board. Only one of them arrived.
Across more than six thousand miles of open ocean, nobody had come for him.
This is the story of José Salvador Alvarenga, and the four hundred and thirty-eight days he spent adrift.
This is No Rescue. True stories of people who survived when no one was coming.

## the fisherman
To understand how a man survives fourteen months at sea, you have to start on the shore he left.
José Salvador Alvarenga was born in El Salvador, in a coastal village called Garita Palmera.
His parents ran a small flour mill and a shop. His father would later say that the sea was always his thing.
As a young man, Alvarenga left home, and crossed the border into Mexico.
He settled on the Pacific coast of Chiapas, and stayed for about fifteen years.
There, he was not called José. The fishermen knew him as Cirilo.
He lived in a fishing village called Costa Azul, a few houses between the lagoons and the open sea.
He fished for sharks, for a boss everybody called Willy.
It was hard, dangerous work. The boats went far offshore, out of sight of land, and came back loaded with fish.
He was good at it. He was strong, and he did not complain.
He had a daughter back in El Salvador, Fátima. She was barely a year old when he left.
For about eight years, he had not spoken to his family.
In November 2012, Alvarenga was in his mid-thirties. And he was about to go out one more time.

## the boat
The boat was a fibreglass skiff, about seven meters long.
It was open to the sky. No cabin, no roof, no shelter of any kind.
It had one outboard engine, and a large plastic ice box, the size of a refrigerator, to keep the catch cold.
On the bow, someone had painted by hand the name of the fishing cooperative. Camaroneros de la Costa.
The trip was supposed to be short. A day or two at sea, chasing sharks, then home.
But his usual fishing partner could not come.
So at the last minute, Alvarenga took a young man from the village, Ezequiel Córdoba.
Ezequiel was twenty-two. He had very little experience at sea. The two men barely knew each other.
Some of the other fishermen had warned that a storm was on its way.
Alvarenga had seen storms before. He calculated he still had time.
On Saturday, the seventeenth of November, 2012, the two men pushed the boat into the water and headed out into the Pacific.

## the norte
Far offshore, the storm arrived. On that coast, they call it a norte.
A wind that pours down from the north, across the mountains, and drives everything on the water out to sea.
The waves rose higher than the boat. Water poured in over the sides.
To stay afloat, they threw their catch overboard. Almost five hundred kilos of fish, everything they had worked for.
Then the engine died.
Alvarenga tried to restart it, again and again. It would not start.
He grabbed the radio, and called the one man he knew would answer. His boss, Willy.
He told him the engine was dead, and begged him to send help.
Then the battery ran out, and the radio went silent.
It was the last time anyone on land would hear his voice for more than a year.
Back in Costa Azul, the alarm was raised. Search planes went up over the coast.
But fog and bad weather closed in. After two days, the official search was called off.
The fishermen kept looking on their own for a few more days.
They found nothing.
By then, the wind and the current had already carried the little boat far beyond the horizon.

## adrift
When the storm finally passed, the two men looked around them.
There was no land. No boats. No aircraft. Nothing in any direction but water.
They had no engine. No sail. No oars. No anchor. No radio.
They had a boat, an empty ice box, a few buckets, and a couple of knives.
And beneath them, without either of them knowing it, the ocean had already chosen their direction.
Just north of the equator, a powerful current flows across the Pacific from east to west.
It is pushed by the trade winds, which blow steadily, day after day, for thousands of miles.
Anything that floats off the coast of Mexico can be caught in it, and carried slowly toward Asia.
A piece of debris. A fishing buoy. Or a small boat with two men inside.
In the first days, the sun was the enemy.
To escape it, they turned the big ice box upside down, and crawled underneath.
Their first water was their own urine.
Then, at last, it rained.
They caught the rain in a bucket, and drank until they could not drink any more.

## the hunt
Alvarenga had spent his life catching fish. Now he had no hooks, no lines and no bait.
So he learned to do it with his bare hands.
He would kneel at the edge of the boat, push his arms into the water, and wait, perfectly still.
When a fish swam close, he closed his fists around it.
Sea turtles came close too. Some of them were strangely calm, and let him lift them into the boat.
He drank their blood. He ate their meat raw.
Seabirds landed on the boat to rest. He caught them, and ate them too.
Jellyfish. Flying fish that landed in the boat. Even sharks.
Once, he told an interviewer, a fish slipped out of his hands. And he cried, because it was gone.
He became a hunter, a fisherman and a butcher, on a boat seven meters long.
And he kept count of time the way his grandfather had taught him. By the moon.
One moon. Then two. Then three.

## ezequiel
Ezequiel Córdoba was not built for this.
He had little experience at sea. And now there was no end to it.
After weeks of raw meat and rainwater, something broke inside him.
According to Alvarenga, the young man fell ill after eating raw seabird.
After that, he stopped eating altogether. He would not touch fish. He would not touch turtle.
Alvarenga tried to make him eat. He told him to hold his nose and swallow.
Ezequiel vomited it back up.
He grew weaker. Thinner. Quieter.
The two men had made each other a promise.
If only one of them survived, he would find the other man's family, and tell them what happened.
In March 2013, about four months after they left Costa Azul, Ezequiel Córdoba died.
Alvarenga was alone.

## six days
He could not let him go.
For six days, he kept his companion's body in the boat, propped up against the bow.
He spoke to him every morning. He told an interviewer years later that he would greet him.
Hello, Ezequiel. How is your morning?
On the sixth day, afraid that he was losing his mind, he let him slip into the sea.

## alone
Now there was only one voice in the boat.
In the days that followed, Alvarenga thought about ending his life.
Later, from a hospital bed, he said it plainly.
I wanted to kill myself, but no. I asked God to save me.
He was a believer, and his faith held him back.
He later told the author Jonathan Franklin how he kept his mind alive.
He would walk back and forth along the boat, and imagine he was wandering the world.
And he told him how hungry he was. So hungry that he chewed his own fingernails, and swallowed every little piece.
And every night, he looked up at the moon.
Four moons. Five. Six.

## the ships
Out there, he was not completely alone.
Over the months, he saw ships.
Huge container vessels crossed his horizon, silent and far away.
He waved. He shouted. Most of them never knew he was there.
After he was found, El Salvador's health minister told reporters what he had told his doctors.
Several ships had passed by him. Some of them close.
Once, the crew of a passing ship looked down at him, waved back, and kept going.
Nobody turned around.
Nobody called it in.
The boat drifted on, through the empty middle of the largest ocean on Earth.

## fifteen moons
Weeks became months. Months became seasons.
The current carried him steadily west, deeper and deeper into the Pacific.
Ten moons. Eleven. Twelve.
In that time, a man on land would have celebrated Christmas, and the New Year, and his birthday.
Alvarenga had no idea what day it was.
He knew only one thing for certain. Every morning, he was still alive.
Thirteen moons. Fourteen.
He survived storms. He survived on rain, on turtle blood, and on the blood of birds.
His body grew thin, his joints swollen. But he did not stop hunting.
Fifteen moons.
And then, one day, at the very end of January 2014, he saw something he had almost forgotten.
On the horizon, a thin line of green.
Palm trees.

## ebon
It was a tiny islet called Tile, on the southern edge of Ebon Atoll, the southernmost land in the Marshall Islands.
A ring of coral and sand barely above the waves, enclosing a lagoon, with a few hundred people living on it.
In 2014, the atoll had one telephone line, and no internet at all.
The current had carried him almost straight to it.
On the night of the twenty-ninth of January, 2014, when the boat was close enough, Alvarenga threw himself into the water, and swam for the shore.
He crawled up the beach. He told Franklin that he held a handful of sand like it was a treasure.
The next morning, a couple who lived alone on the islet saw him. Emi Libokmeto and Russel Laikidrik.
A wild-looking man, wearing almost nothing, holding a knife, shouting words they did not understand.
They told him to put the knife away. And then they took him in.
When the mayor of Ebon came to see the boat, she described what she found inside.
Bird bones. Feathers. The small shell of a turtle.
And one bird, still alive, tied to the boat by its leg.
For four hundred and thirty-eight days, nobody had come for him.
In the end, he had come to them.

## nobody believed
A few days later, a government patrol boat carried him to Majuro, the capital of the Marshall Islands. The journey took almost a full day.
Doctors found him dehydrated, anaemic, with swollen legs and aching joints. He would spend eleven days under their care.
Within days, his story was everywhere.
A fisherman who had drifted from Mexico to the Marshall Islands. More than six thousand miles in a broken boat.
Many people did not believe a word of it.
A senior Marshall Islands official told reporters he was not sure he believed the story.
The American ambassador said it was hard to imagine anyone surviving that long at sea.
He was not skeletal, as people expected. He was weak, swollen and limping, but strong.
The newspapers compared him to Tom Hanks in Cast Away.
But piece by piece, the story held.
In Chiapas, the civil protection authorities confirmed that a boat with two fishermen had been reported missing from Costa Azul in November 2012.
The boat on the beach carried the name of the cooperative painted on its bow.
Fishermen back home recognised him, and said they had searched for him.
Then the scientists came in.

## the current
At the University of Hawaii, oceanographers Nikolai Maximenko and Jan Hafner had spent years tracking debris across the Pacific.
After the tsunami in Japan in 2011, they had built a computer model of how floating objects travel across the ocean.
Now they used it to test Alvarenga's story.
They released sixteen virtual boats off the coast of Chiapas, and let the winds and currents of those months carry them.
Most of them drifted west, in a long arc across the Pacific.
And after about thirteen months, the ones that came closest to the Marshall Islands passed within about one hundred and twenty miles of Ebon.
Another oceanographer, Erik van Sebille, said a journey like that would usually take a year and a half to two years.
Thirteen or fourteen months was fast. But it was possible.
The model did not prove the story. But it showed that the ocean could have done exactly what he said.
Two months later, his lawyer announced that Alvarenga had passed a lie detector test.
And had he missed that tiny atoll, the current would have carried him on, toward the Philippines, thousands of miles further, into the open ocean.

## after
On the eleventh of February, 2014, José Salvador Alvarenga landed in San Salvador.
He arrived in a wheelchair, too exhausted to speak.
In Garita Palmera, his parents had believed he was dead.
His daughter, Fátima, was now fourteen. She did not remember her father.
A month later, he kept his promise.
He travelled to Chiapas, to the family of Ezequiel Córdoba, to tell them how their son had died.
He said that whenever he talked about that day, he felt he was back in the moment, suffering again.
But the story did not end there.
In 2015, a lawsuit was filed in the name of Ezequiel's family, demanding one million dollars.
It accused Alvarenga of eating his companion to survive.
Alvarenga denied it completely. He said he had promised Ezequiel he would never do that.
No evidence was ever produced. Ezequiel's mother said she had never filed the lawsuit herself.
Alvarenga lives with that question to this day. He said once that people will keep asking him, maybe until he dies.
He was diagnosed with a fear of the sea. He had nightmares about the water.
The man who had lived on the ocean all his life could no longer look at it.

## no rescue
Here is what makes this story so hard to believe.
José Salvador Alvarenga had no survival training. No equipment. No food. No water.
He had a broken boat, an upside-down ice box, his bare hands, and the moon.
Ships passed him by. A search was called off after two days. The whole world had forgotten him.
He lost the only other person on the boat, and he went on alone.
For four hundred and thirty-eight days, he did the same thing every single morning.
He woke up, and he chose to keep going.
In the end, there was no rescue.
There was only a fisherman, a current, and a line of palm trees on the horizon.
