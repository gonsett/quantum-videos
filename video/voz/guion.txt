## the new deal
Here is the part most fans never saw.
For almost a century, every World Cup worked the same way. The host country set up a local organizing committee.
That committee ran the tournament on the ground, and it shared in the money. Tickets. Local sponsors. Sometimes, even a cut of the food.
In 1994, the American organizers ran the ticketing themselves, and the tournament ended with a surplus that helped build soccer in the United States.
In 2026, that model was gone.
For the first time, there was no local organizing committee with its own share of the money. FIFA ran the tournament itself, through its own subsidiary.
The television rights. The global sponsors. The tickets. The hospitality suites. The merchandise. All of it went to FIFA.
One business magazine described it as a franchise model. FIFA owns the brand, and the host cities run the stores.
But in this franchise, the stores pay for the rent, the security, and the electricity. And the brand keeps the sales.
The contracts the cities signed spelled it out. Security. Transport. Fan festivals. Stadium upgrades. Those were the host city's job.
FIFA also asked for something else. Broad exemptions from taxes. In Mexico, the government granted them. In the United States, ticket sales in host cities went untaxed.
In the ledger, a second line. A tournament with no local partner. And every stream of revenue, flowing to one place.
## the tickets
Which brings us to the most visible part of the business. The tickets.
For the first time at a World Cup, FIFA used dynamic pricing. The same system airlines and concert promoters use.
The price isn't fixed. It moves with demand. If a match is popular, the price goes up. And up.
FIFA advertised that tickets started at sixty dollars. In practice, those seats were few, and mostly sold through national federations, to their most loyal fans.
After heavy criticism, FIFA promised a thousand seats at that price for every match. In stadiums that hold sixty, seventy, eighty thousand people.
For the final, the numbers got surreal.
A top category seat at the final in Qatar, in 2022, cost about sixteen hundred dollars.
In 2026, FIFA's best category was first listed at just under eleven thousand.
Then, in May, FIFA tripled it. Thirty two thousand, nine hundred and seventy dollars. For one seat. At face value.
FIFA's president defended the prices. In the United States, he argued, you have to apply market rates.
The consumer groups were less impressed. Supporters' organizations calculated that following one team, from its first match to the final, would cost at least six thousand nine hundred dollars. In tickets alone.
Before a single flight, hotel, or meal.
And it didn't end there. The attorneys general of New York and New Jersey opened an investigation into how the tickets were priced, and how the seats were described.

## the resale
But the most elegant part of the business happened after the ticket was sold.
FIFA ran its own official resale platform. If you couldn't go, you could sell your ticket there. If you missed out, you could buy one.
And on every resale, FIFA took a cut. Fifteen percent from the buyer. And fifteen percent from the seller.
Think about what that means. The same seat can be sold three, four, five times. And every time it changes hands, the organizer gets paid again.
In the United States and Canada, there was no cap on resale prices. So the higher the market went, the bigger FIFA's slice.
In other words, FIFA didn't just sell the ticket. It owned the toll booth on every road that ticket could travel.
In the ledger, a fourth line. Thirty percent on every resale. A ticket that pays its seller again, and again.
## the bill
Now let's look at the other side of the ledger. The people who paid.
Hosting a World Cup match is not cheap. Police, emergency services, transport, traffic control, cleaning, a fan festival for tens of thousands of people.
Host cities estimated the bill at between one hundred and two hundred million dollars each.
The federal government stepped in with six hundred and twenty five million dollars for security. Split across eleven cities, that helped. But it didn't cover everything.
Kansas City reported a security gap of tens of millions of dollars.
In New York, the city's own comptroller estimated that the tournament would bring in less money than it cost to host.
In Toronto, the price of hosting climbed into the hundreds of millions.
Some cities spent around a million dollars a day, just to run their fan festivals.
And remember, the biggest revenue of the event, the tickets, went to FIFA. Not to the city that paid for the police outside the stadium.
One sports economist put it simply. When you spend four hundred dollars on a World Cup ticket, that money goes to FIFA.
In the ledger, a fifth line. The security, the roads, the trains, and the parties. Paid for by the people who live there.
## the boom
But wait, the cities said. The money will come back. In tourism. In hotels. In restaurants.
And FIFA had a number ready. A study it commissioned predicted the tournament would add tens of billions of dollars to the North American economy.
Economists who study these events have heard that promise before. One academic review found that most recent World Cups ended with net economic losses for their hosts.
And in 2026, the hotels told the story early.
In May, a survey by the American hotel industry found that most hotels in host cities were seeing bookings below expectations. Around eight in ten.
Part of the reason was FIFA itself. It had reserved blocks of rooms in advance. Then it released many of them back to the market.
Part of it was the cost of travel. Visa fees went up. Flights were expensive. And many international fans simply stayed home.
When the tournament came, the data was mixed. Some host cities saw record room rates on match days. In New York, one night set an all time record for a host market.
But in other cities, normal visitors stayed away. Business travelers, conventions, regular tourists, all avoided the chaos.
In Seattle, hotel occupancy fell more than in any other major market.
Across July, the number of international visitors to the United States barely moved. Overseas arrivals were actually down.
The industry's verdict was clear. The World Cup was a story about prices, not about crowds.
In the ledger, a sixth line. A boom that was promised. And fewer guests than expected.
## the stadium
And then there was the experience of actually getting to a game.
Take the final. The stadium in New Jersey sits in a marsh, a few miles from Manhattan.
New Jersey Transit announced a round trip train ticket of a hundred and fifty dollars. For a ride that normally costs a fraction of that.
After a public outcry, the price came down, to just under a hundred.
The rail agency explained why it was so expensive. Running the trains for the tournament cost tens of millions of dollars. And only a small part of that was covered by FIFA or the organizers.
Driving wasn't much better. Parking passes near the stadium went for over two hundred dollars. And tailgating, the great American tradition, was banned.
Inside, a beer cost around thirteen dollars.
None of this was illegal. None of it was hidden. It was simply the business model, applied to every step between your front door and your seat.
In the ledger, a seventh line. A train ride, a parking spot, a beer. Each one, priced like a final.

## the total
Now, let's add up FIFA's side.
FIFA works in four year cycles. Each cycle is built around one men's World Cup.
For the cycle that ended with this tournament, FIFA's original budget was eleven billion dollars. It was later raised to thirteen.
The day before the final, FIFA's president said he believed the total would top fifteen billion dollars.
For comparison, the cycle around the World Cup in Qatar brought in about seven and a half billion.
So in one tournament, FIFA roughly doubled its money.
According to reports in the British press, tickets and hospitality alone may have brought in around five billion dollars. For a single tournament. More than five times what Qatar made from tickets.
FIFA will publish its official figures in its next annual report. But even the conservative numbers are records.
And because FIFA is a non profit association, it doesn't pay dividends to shareholders. The money goes back into football. Development programs for its two hundred and eleven member federations. Two point seven billion dollars promised for the next four years.

## the verdict
So let's close the books.
A tournament that, for the first time, had no local partner to share the money.
