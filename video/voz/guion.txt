## the question
In November 1956, a small science fiction magazine published a story by Isaac Asimov.
It was called, The Last Question.
It begins in the year 2061, with two technicians, slightly drunk, standing next to a giant computer.
They are celebrating. Humanity has just learned to draw its energy directly from the Sun. Enough energy, they think, to last forever.
But one of them is not so sure. The Sun will not last forever, he says. Nothing will.
So, half as a joke, they ask the computer a question. Can the decline of the universe be reversed?
The machine goes quiet. It thinks. And then it answers.
Insufficient data for meaningful answer.
The story then jumps forward. Thousands of years. Millions. Billions. Trillions.
Each time, humanity has changed beyond recognition. And each time, someone asks the very same question.
Asimov later said it was his favourite of all the stories he ever wrote.
Because the question in that story is not fiction. It is one of the deepest problems in physics. And it was first asked, seriously, a century before Asimov.
In 1852, the physicist William Thomson, who would later become Lord Kelvin, published a short paper with a very dry title.
On a universal tendency in nature to the dissipation of mechanical energy.
His conclusion was anything but dry.
[bm_george:en-gb] Within a finite period of time to come, the earth must again be unfit for the habitation of man as at present constituted.
Thirteen years later, the German physicist Rudolf Clausius gave the idea its name. Entropy.
Entropy is, roughly speaking, a measure of how spread out energy is. Of how much disorder there is in a system.
And Clausius summed up its law in a single sentence.
[bm_george:en-gb] The entropy of the universe tends to a maximum.
Every star that shines, every engine that runs, every heartbeat, takes concentrated energy and spreads it out.
And it never goes the other way on its own.
Ice melts in a warm room. A cup of coffee cools down. It never heats itself back up.
And the universe, slowly, is cooling down too.
The end point of that process has a name that sounds almost gentle. The heat death.
Not a fire. Not an explosion. Just a universe where everything is the same temperature, and nothing can ever happen again.
But how long does it take to get there? What happens along the way? And is that really the only way the story can end?
For most of the twentieth century, nobody could say.
Then, in 1997, two young physicists at the University of Michigan did something no one had quite done before.
Fred Adams and Greg Laughlin sat down and worked out the entire future of the universe. Step by step. From now, until the end.
Their paper was called, A Dying Universe. And it is the map we will follow tonight.
## the clock
To read that map, we first need a new kind of clock.
The universe is about thirteen point eight billion years old. That sounds like an unimaginably long time.
It is not.
Adams and Laughlin realised that ordinary numbers are almost useless for the future. So they counted time differently. Not in years, but in powers of ten.
They called these steps, cosmological decades.
Ten years is one decade. A hundred years is two. A thousand years is three.
Each step on this clock is ten times longer than the step before it.
Human history, from the first cities to today, fits inside decade four.
The dinosaurs lived and died within decade eight.
The Sun and the Earth were born in decade nine.
And everything that has ever happened, the Big Bang, the first stars, the first cells, every human being who has ever lived, all of it fits inside the first ten decades.
The future, as we will see, runs to a hundred decades. And possibly, far beyond.
Here is another way to feel it.
Imagine squeezing the whole lifetime of the universe, until its last black hole is gone, into a single year.
On that calendar, the entire history of the cosmos so far, all thirteen point eight billion years, would last less than the shortest moment physics can even describe.
Less than a flicker. Less than a heartbeat of a heartbeat.
In other words, the universe has barely been born. Almost everything that will ever happen has not happened yet.
We are not living near the end of the story. We are living in the first line of the first page.
So let us start the clock. And let us start close to home.
## the last day of earth
Our Sun is about four and a half billion years old. It is roughly halfway through its life as a normal star.
It shines by fusing hydrogen into helium deep in its core.
As the helium builds up, the core slowly contracts, and heats up. And so the Sun gets brighter.
Very slowly. About one percent every hundred million years.
That does not sound like much. But the climate of the Earth sits on a knife edge.
In around a billion years, the extra sunlight will push our planet past a tipping point.
The oceans will begin to evaporate. Water vapour traps heat, which evaporates more water, which traps more heat.
Long before the Sun dies, the Earth will become a dry and scorching world. A second Venus.
Life may hold on for a while, deep underground, or in the last shrinking pools. But the blue planet, as we know it, has an expiry date.
Even the sky will change.
The Moon is slowly drifting away from us, a few centimetres every year.
Today, by a beautiful coincidence, it looks almost exactly the same size as the Sun. That is why we have total solar eclipses.
In several hundred million years, the Moon will have drifted too far to cover the Sun completely. The last total eclipse will happen, and no one may be there to see it.
Then, about five billion years from now, the Sun will run out of hydrogen in its core.
And it will do something dramatic.
Its core will contract and heat up. And its outer layers will swell. Out past Mercury. Out past Venus.
The Sun will become a red giant. Hundreds of times wider than it is today.
Whether it swallows the Earth is still debated.
As the Sun swells, it blows away part of its own mass. Its grip weakens, and the planets drift outward.
In 2008, two astronomers, Klaus-Peter Schröder and Robert Connon Smith, ran the numbers in detail.
Their conclusion. The Earth probably does not escape.
The bloated Sun raises a tide on our planet, and that tide drags the Earth inward, until it spirals into the Sun's outer atmosphere. And is gone.
Then the giant will exhale.
Its outer layers will drift away into space, glowing for a few thousand years as a planetary nebula. Like these.
They are some of the most beautiful objects in the sky. And every one of them is the last breath of a star like ours.
What remains at the centre is the Sun's naked core. A white dwarf.
About half the mass of the Sun, crushed into a ball the size of the Earth.
A single teaspoon of it would weigh several tonnes.
It will not burn anything. It will simply glow, with the heat it has left. And it will spend the rest of eternity cooling down.
That is the fate of our Sun. But by then, the sky above the solar system will look completely different.
## milkomeda
On a clear autumn night, far from city lights, you can see a faint smudge in the sky.
It is the Andromeda Galaxy. Hundreds of billions of stars, two and a half million light years away.
It is the most distant thing most people will ever see with their own eyes.
And it is falling towards us, at about a hundred and ten kilometres every second.
In 2012, astronomers used the Hubble Space Telescope to measure Andromeda's sideways motion across the sky for the first time.
The answer seemed clear. A head-on collision. In about four billion years, our two galaxies would crash into each other.
NASA even released images of what our night sky would look like, as Andromeda grew to fill it.
For more than a decade, that was the textbook story.
Then, in 2025, a team led by Till Sawala ran the numbers again. With new data from the Gaia and Hubble space telescopes. And this time, with the other large galaxies in our neighbourhood included.
They found that the collision is far from certain.
In only about half of their simulations do the Milky Way and Andromeda merge within the next ten billion years. And the chance of a collision in the next five billion is only about two percent.
It is a good reminder of what science really is. Not a list of fixed answers, but our best measurement, today. Open to the next one.
But in the very long run, gravity is patient.
Our galaxy and Andromeda are bound to each other. Over enough time, they will become one. Astronomers already have a name for it. Milkomeda.
And even then, almost no stars will collide.
The space between stars is so vast that two galaxies can pass right through each other, like two clouds of dust that never touch.
What changes is the shape. Two graceful spirals become a single, vast, featureless ball of old stars.
And far beyond it, something else will be happening. Something much stranger.
## the lonely sky
In 1998, two teams of astronomers were studying exploding stars in distant galaxies.
They expected to find that the expansion of the universe was slowing down, held back by gravity.
Instead, they found it was speeding up.
Something, which we now call dark energy, is pushing space apart. Faster and faster.
The discovery won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2011. And it has a consequence that is almost poetic.
As space expands faster and faster, distant galaxies are carried away from us faster than their light can cross the gap.
One by one, they slip over the horizon. And their light will never reach us again.
In 2007, two physicists, Lawrence Krauss and Robert Scherrer, worked out what that means for the astronomers of the far future.
Look at this image. It is a tiny patch of sky, smaller than a grain of sand held at arm's length. And almost every point of light in it is a galaxy.
In about a hundred billion years, every one of those galaxies will be gone.
Every galaxy beyond our own small group will have slipped out of sight. The night sky of Milkomeda will contain exactly one galaxy. Itself.
Surrounded by endless, empty dark.
And the afterglow of the Big Bang, the faint microwave light that fills the whole sky today, will have been stretched and faded until it can no longer be detected at all.
Now think about what that means.
An astronomer living then, with telescopes far better than ours, would look out and see one island of stars, floating in a static, unchanging void.
They would have no way of knowing that the universe is expanding. No way of knowing there was a Big Bang. No trace of the hundreds of billions of other galaxies.
The evidence will not be hidden. It will be gone.
Krauss and Scherrer put it in a single, haunting line.
[bm_george:en-gb] We are living at a very special time in the evolution of the universe.
We live in a lucky moment. Early enough to see the whole universe, and to read its history in the light.
The astronomers of the future will not be so lucky. They will reach the wrong conclusion. And they will have no way to know it.
## the last stars
Now let us turn the clock forward. Past ten billion years. Past a hundred billion. Past a trillion.
The great stars are long gone.
The brilliant blue giants that light up the arms of galaxies live fast. A few million years, and then they explode.
Stars like our Sun last around ten billion years.
But the real survivors of the universe are smaller. Much smaller.
Red dwarfs. Stars with a tenth of the Sun's mass, or even less.
They are dim and cool, and so common that roughly three out of every four stars in our galaxy is one of them.
The closest star to the Sun, Proxima Centauri, is a red dwarf.
A red dwarf sips its fuel so slowly that it can shine for trillions of years.
Not a single red dwarf has ever died. The universe is simply not old enough.
In 1997, Greg Laughlin and his colleagues simulated how these tiny stars will grow old.
And they found something no one had ever seen.
As a red dwarf nears the end of its life, it does not swell into a giant. It shrinks a little, and grows hotter, and bluer.
It becomes a blue dwarf. A kind of star that does not exist yet. Anywhere in the cosmos.
Meanwhile, the raw material for new stars is running out.
Every generation of stars locks more gas away inside dead remnants. Less and less is left over to make new ones.
And over roughly a hundred trillion years, star formation stops for good.
Somewhere, a final blue dwarf will flicker, fade, and cool into darkness.
And after that, no star will ever shine again.
Adams and Laughlin call the age we live in now, the Stelliferous Era. The age of stars.
It began a few hundred million years after the Big Bang. And at a hundred trillion years, ten to the power of fourteen, it ends.
On our cosmic clock, that is decade fourteen. Out of a hundred.
The age of light, the only age that life as we know it could ever exist in, is a thin sliver at the very start of time.
What comes next is not empty. It is full of the dead.
## the degenerate era
The universe after the stars is a graveyard.
Brown dwarfs, the failed stars that never managed to ignite. White dwarfs. Neutron stars. And black holes.
Adams and Laughlin named it the Degenerate Era, after the strange, crushed state of the matter inside these remnants.
Remember the white dwarf that our Sun will become.
A white dwarf takes far longer than the current age of the universe to cool down completely. That is why not a single black dwarf exists today.
In the Degenerate Era, they will be everywhere. Cold, dark spheres of carbon and oxygen, drifting through the void.
And yet, even here, there will be flashes of light.
Every so often, two brown dwarfs will collide. And if their combined mass is large enough, a brand new star will ignite.
For a few trillion years, that one small star may be the only light shining in its entire galaxy.
Over immense spans of time, the galaxies themselves will come apart.
Every time two dead stars pass close to each other, one is flung outward, and the other sinks deeper.
The planets go first. Passing stars will tear them away from their orbits, and send them wandering alone through the dark.
Then the stars. Encounter after encounter, over around ten to the power of nineteen years, most of them will be flung out of their galaxies altogether.
And the few that are left behind will slowly spiral into the black hole at the centre.
And then comes the deepest question of this era.
Do atoms last forever?
Every atom in your body has a nucleus, built from protons. And as far as anyone has ever been able to tell, protons never decay.
But many of the theories that try to unite the forces of nature predict that they must. Eventually.
To find out, physicists built one of the strangest experiments on Earth.
Super-Kamiokande. A tank holding fifty thousand tonnes of ultra-pure water, a kilometre under a mountain in Japan, lined with more than eleven thousand light detectors.
The idea is simple. If you watch enough protons, for long enough, you might catch one falling apart.
After decades of watching, they have never seen it happen.
That tells us that the average proton lives for more than ten to the power of thirty-four years. Nearly a trillion trillion times longer than the universe has existed so far.
But if protons do decay, even that slowly, then the Degenerate Era has an end.
Somewhere between ten to the power of thirty-seven and ten to the power of forty years from now, the dead stars will finally evaporate. Particle by particle.
White dwarfs, neutron stars, lost planets. The atoms of everything that ever lived. Dissolved into a thin mist of light, and electrons, and their antimatter twins.
When the last of ordinary matter is gone, only one kind of object will remain.
The black hole.
## black holes
For a long time, black holes were thought to be the one thing in the universe that could only grow.
Anything that falls in, never comes out.
Then, in 1974, a thirty-two year old physicist named Stephen Hawking published a short paper in the journal Nature.
Its title was a question. Black hole explosions?
Hawking had combined Einstein's theory of gravity with quantum mechanics. And he found that black holes are not completely black.
They glow. Very, very faintly.
They give off a trickle of radiation, now called Hawking radiation. And as they do, they lose energy, and mass.
Slowly, impossibly slowly, they evaporate.
And there is a twist. The bigger the black hole, the colder it is, and the slower it evaporates.
Today, this makes no difference at all.
A black hole with the mass of the Sun is far colder than the faint afterglow of the Big Bang. It absorbs more than it gives off. It is still growing.
In 2019, we saw the shadow of one for the first time. This is the black hole at the heart of the galaxy M87. Six and a half billion times the mass of the Sun.
But in the far future, when the universe has cooled below even the coldest black hole, the balance tips. And the black holes begin to shrink.
For a black hole with the mass of our Sun, it takes around ten to the power of sixty-seven years to evaporate completely.
For the giants, the supermassive black holes at the hearts of galaxies, it takes far longer.
Around ten to the power of a hundred years. A one, followed by a hundred zeros.
A number so large that it has its own name. A googol.
This is the Black Hole Era. And it is by far the longest age of the universe that contains anything at all.
For an unimaginable stretch of time, black holes will be the only objects left. Islands of darkness, in a darkness that is almost total.
And at the very end of its life, every black hole does something extraordinary.
As it shrinks, it gets hotter. And the hotter it gets, the faster it shrinks.
In its final moments, it releases everything it has left, in a burst of high-energy radiation. A flash.
The last black hole in the universe will end its life the way the universe began. In a brief, brilliant flash of light.
Somewhere around a googol years from now, it will be the final event that deserves to be called an event.
## the dark era
And then. Nothing much.
After the last black hole is gone, the universe enters what Adams and Laughlin called, the Dark Era.
There are no stars. No planets. No atoms, in any meaningful sense.
Just a thin haze of photons, and neutrinos, and electrons, and positrons. Spread across a volume of space that words cannot describe.
Now and then, an electron and a positron will find each other, and begin to orbit. A ghostly kind of atom, wider than the entire observable universe is today.
They will circle each other for an absurd length of time, before they finally meet, and vanish.
This is the heat death that Kelvin imagined.
Not hot. Not cold. Just uniform. A universe at maximum entropy, where there is no difference left to use. No energy flowing from one place to another.
And without a flow of energy, nothing can happen. No chemistry. No motion that means anything. No thought.
But is that really the end?
In 1979, the physicist Freeman Dyson published a paper called Time Without End.
He asked a provocative question. Could life, or intelligence, survive forever, even in a dying universe?
His answer was a cautious yes.
A mind, he argued, could slow its thinking down as the universe cooled. It could hibernate for longer and longer stretches, spending less and less energy.
And with a finite supply of energy, it could, in principle, go on thinking forever.
Dyson also worked out what happens to matter if protons never decay.
Over ten to the power of fifteen hundred years, quantum effects would slowly rearrange every dead star, until it becomes a perfect, cold sphere of iron.
Later physicists, after dark energy was discovered, argued that even Dyson's strategy eventually fails. In an accelerating universe, there may simply not be enough to work with.
And there is one more way the universe could end. One that could arrive long before any of this.
The Higgs field, the field that gives particles their mass, may not be in its most stable state.
If our universe sits in what physicists call a false vacuum, then somewhere, someday, by pure chance, a bubble of true vacuum could appear.
It would expand at close to the speed of light, rewriting the laws of physics inside it. Nothing would see it coming.
The estimates of how long that might take are enormously uncertain. But they are enormous, too. Far beyond the age of black holes.
So, probably, nothing to lose sleep over.
## other endings
Everything we have described so far rests on one assumption. That dark energy stays exactly as it is.
And that is not guaranteed.
If dark energy grows stronger over time, the expansion would keep accelerating, without limit.
In 2003, three physicists, Robert Caldwell, Marc Kamionkowski and Nevin Weinberg, described what would happen. They called it, the Big Rip.
First, galaxies are pulled apart. Then solar systems. Then the planets themselves. And in the final moments, atoms are torn to pieces.
In their example, it would happen about twenty-two billion years from now.
But for decades, the measurements showed no sign of that. Dark energy looked remarkably constant.
Or at least, it did.
In 2024 and 2025, a new instrument called DESI, the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument, mapped the positions of millions of galaxies, across billions of years of cosmic history.
And its results hinted at something nobody expected. Dark energy may be getting weaker.
The evidence is not yet conclusive. But if it is true, it could change the ending completely.
If dark energy weakens enough, the expansion could one day slow down, stop, and reverse.
The universe would fall back in on itself. A Big Crunch.
In 2025, a team of physicists including Henry Tye of Cornell University fitted a model to the new data.
In their model, the universe lives for about thirty-three billion years in total. Its expansion peaks soon, and the end comes about twenty billion years from now.
It is one model, built on early data. Most cosmologists are waiting for more measurements before they believe anything like it.
But it shows how much is still unknown.
The ending of the universe is not yet written. It depends on the nature of something that makes up about two thirds of everything there is. And we still do not know what it is.
## let there be light
Let us go back to Asimov's story.
In each new era, humanity grows more powerful. It spreads across the galaxy. Then across the universe. It merges with its own machines.
And each time, it asks the same question. Can entropy be reversed?
And each time, the answer is the same. Insufficient data for meaningful answer.
Finally, the stars go out. Humanity merges with its last computer, which now exists outside of space and time.
And there is nothing left. Except the question.
The computer keeps thinking. For an eternity, it gathers data, and puts it together. Until at last, it learns how to reverse entropy.
But there is no one left to tell.
So it decides to show them instead. And Asimov ends his story with four words.
Let there be light.
It is a beautiful ending. And for a long time, physicists would have called it pure fiction.
Then, a Nobel laureate suggested something remarkably similar.
Roger Penrose won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2020, for his work on black holes. And for years, he has argued for an idea called conformal cyclic cosmology.
Penrose noticed something strange about the far future.
When the last black holes have evaporated, almost everything left is light, and other particles without mass.
And massless things have no sense of time. A ray of light carries no clock. It has no way to measure distance either.
In a universe made only of light, Penrose argues, there is no way to tell the difference between something enormous and something tiny.
The infinitely large, cold, empty future becomes mathematically identical to the infinitely small, hot, dense beginning.
The end of one universe becomes the Big Bang of the next.
Penrose even believes he has found evidence. Faint warm spots in the afterglow of our own Big Bang, which he calls Hawking points.
The last flashes of black holes, he suggests, from a universe that came before ours.
Most cosmologists are not convinced. Other teams, analysing the same data, have found no clear signal at all. It remains a minority view, and it may well be wrong.
But it means the question Asimov asked in 1956 is still open.
We know, with remarkable confidence, how the stars will die. We know the order of the eras, and roughly how long each one will last.
What we do not know, is whether the end is really the end.
Maybe the universe will simply fade, forever. A googol years of darkness, and then a googol more.
Or maybe, at the very end, when there is no one left to see it, something will happen that we cannot yet imagine.
For now, there is only one honest answer. The same one Asimov's computer gave, again and again.
Insufficient data for meaningful answer.
And yet.
Here we are, at the very dawn of time, in the brief age of stars.
On a small planet, circling an ordinary star, a species that has existed for a few hundred thousand years has worked out the future of the entire universe.
Using nothing but light, and mathematics, and curiosity.
Whatever happens at the end, that is not nothing.
And if the end really is a new beginning, then the next question is obvious.
What came before the Big Bang?
