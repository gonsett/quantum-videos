## gancho
Yaviza, Panama. A small town on a brown river, at the edge of the jungle.
Here, the road just ends.
Behind you, the asphalt runs north without a single break. Through Central America, through Mexico, the United States and Canada, all the way to the Arctic Ocean, in Alaska.
Ahead of you, in Colombia, the same road begins again. And it runs south, down the Andes, all the way to the bottom of Argentina.
Together, they are the Pan-American Highway. About thirty thousand kilometers. The longest road on Earth.
And it has exactly one gap. Right here.
About a hundred kilometers of swamp, mountains and rainforest that no road has ever crossed.
It's called the Darién Gap.
For five hundred years, it has swallowed conquistadors, colonists, explorers and armies.
Engineers have tried to cross it. Presidents have promised to pave it. A judge in Washington stopped it.
And in 2023, more than half a million people walked through it. On foot.
This is the story of the only gap in the longest road on Earth. And of why, after a century of trying, nobody has closed it.

To understand the gap, you first have to understand the road.
## ruta
In 1923, in Santiago de Chile, the countries of the Americas agreed on a dream. One road, to tie the whole hemisphere together.
They called it the Pan-American Highway.
It was never really one road. It's a chain of national highways, each one built by its own country, and joined at the borders.
In the north, it starts at Prudhoe Bay, Alaska, on the Arctic Ocean.
In the south, it ends near Ushuaia, at the end of the world.
In between, it crosses deserts, volcanoes, the Andes, and more than a dozen countries.
Guinness World Records calls it the longest motorable road in the world.
And over the decades, almost every piece of it was built. Mexico finished its part. Central America finished its part. Colombia, Peru, Chile and Argentina finished theirs.
Except one.
Look at Panama. The highway comes down from Costa Rica, crosses the Panama Canal, and heads east.
Then, at Yaviza, it stops.
On the other side, in Colombia, the road picks up again near the Gulf of Urabá.
Between them, about a hundred kilometers on the map.
On the ground, there is nothing. No asphalt. No bridge. Not even a dirt track for a truck.
If you want to drive from Alaska to Argentina, this is where you stop, put your car in a shipping container, and send it around by sea.
So why? Why would the most connected century in history leave this one hole open?
The answer is the map. And it starts with what's inside the hole.
## selva
About three million years ago, this land did not exist. North and South America were separate continents, with an open sea between them.
Then the sea floor rose, volcanoes grew, and a bridge of land closed the gap. The Isthmus of Panama.
Animals poured across it in both directions. Armadillos and opossums went north. Cats, bears and horses went south. Scientists call it the Great American Interchange.
The Darién is the last link of that bridge. The place where Central America meets South America.
And it seems built to be impassable.
On the Panamanian side, there are mountain ridges, covered in rainforest so thick that sunlight barely reaches the ground.
On the Colombian side, there is the Atrato River. And around its mouth, huge swamps and flooded forest that swallow anything heavier than a canoe.
The Chocó, just south of here, is one of the rainiest places on the planet. Some towns get more than ten meters of rain a year.
Rivers rise in hours. Trails turn to mud. What was a path in the morning can be a torrent by the afternoon.
And the life inside is extraordinary. Jaguars. Harpy eagles. Poison dart frogs. Hundreds of species of birds.
In 1981, UNESCO declared Darién National Park a World Heritage Site. More than five thousand square kilometers of forest.
In 1994, Los Katíos National Park, right across the border in Colombia, joined the list too.
And the Darién is not empty. For centuries, the Emberá, the Wounaan and the Guna peoples have lived along these rivers. Traveling the way the jungle allows. By dugout canoe.
For them, the Darién is not a gap. It's home.
For almost everyone else, it has been a graveyard.
## conquista
Strange as it sounds, the Darién was one of the first places on the American mainland that Europeans tried to settle.
In 1510, Spanish conquistadors founded a town here, near the Caribbean coast. Santa María la Antigua del Darién.
It was one of the first European cities on the mainland of the Americas.
Its most famous resident had arrived as a stowaway, hidden, so the story goes, in a barrel, to escape his debts. Vasco Núñez de Balboa.
In September 1513, Balboa marched into the jungle with about a hundred and ninety Spaniards, and hundreds of Indigenous guides and porters.
Weeks later, from the top of a ridge, he became the first European to see the Pacific Ocean from the Americas.
On the twenty-ninth, he waded into the water, holding a banner and a sword. And he claimed the entire ocean, and every land it touched, for the King of Spain.
Six years later, Balboa was beheaded, accused of treason by his rival, the governor Pedrarias Dávila.
And Santa María was abandoned. Within a few years, the jungle had taken it back.
The Spanish moved to a new town on the Pacific. They called it Panama.
For the next three centuries, the silver and gold of the Andes crossed through Panama. Not through the Darién.
## escoceses
But the Darién still had one more nation to break. And this one was Scotland.
In the 1690s, Scotland was poor, hungry, and an independent kingdom. It shared a king with England, but almost nothing else.
A Scottish merchant named William Paterson, one of the founders of the Bank of England, had an idea. A Scottish colony on the Darién. A trading post between two oceans.
[am_fenrir:en-us] The door of the seas, and the key of the universe.
That's how he described it. And Scotland believed him.
Nobles, merchants, towns and ordinary families put their savings into the Company of Scotland.
It raised around four hundred thousand pounds. By different estimates, somewhere between a fifth and half of all the capital in the country.
In July 1698, five ships left Leith, near Edinburgh, with about twelve hundred people on board.
In November, they reached the Caribbean coast of the Darién, and named their new home Caledonia.
They built a fort, Fort Saint Andrew, and planned a town, New Edinburgh.
The Guna were friendly. Everything else was not.
Their ships were full of wigs, combs, mirrors and Bibles. Nobody in the jungle wanted them.
Then the rains came. And with them, fever.
And King William, who ruled Scotland too but did not want trouble with Spain, ordered his English colonies in the Caribbean not to help them.
In the summer of 1699, eight months after landing, the survivors abandoned Caledonia.
A second fleet, which had sailed before the news reached Scotland, arrived to find empty huts and graves.
Months later, the Spanish surrounded them. In the spring of 1700, the last Scots surrendered and sailed away.
Of about two and a half thousand people who sailed to the Darién, around two thousand never came home.
Scotland was ruined.
Seven years later, in 1707, Scotland signed the Act of Union with England, and Great Britain was born.
Inside the treaty was a payment called the Equivalent. Almost four hundred thousand pounds. And a large part of it went to pay back the investors in the Darién.
A swamp in the Darién helped end an independent kingdom.
## strain
In the 1850s, the world wanted a canal. And on paper, the Darién looked perfect.
An Irish doctor named Edward Cullen claimed he had crossed it several times, easily, over land that never rose above a hundred and fifty feet.
In January 1854, the United States Navy sent an expedition to follow his route. Twenty-seven men, led by Lieutenant Isaac Strain.
They carried food for only a few days.
There was no easy pass. There was no path at all. The maps were wrong.
They got lost in the mountains. They ran out of food. They ate palm nuts, and whatever they could catch.
Their few days in the jungle became more than two months.
At least six of them never came out.
Strain survived. But his health never recovered, and he died a few years later, at thirty-six.
After that, the dream of a canal moved west, to the narrow waist of Panama. And the Darién was left alone.
## cruces
Then came the automobile.
Through the 1930s, 40s and 50s, the Pan-American Highway crept south through Central America, much of it paid for by the United States.
And in 1960, a small team decided to prove that a car could cross the Darién.
They had a Land Rover and a Jeep. Among them was a Panamanian couple. Amado Araúz, and his wife, the anthropologist Reina Torres de Araúz.
They nicknamed the Land Rover La Cucaracha Cariñosa. The Affectionate Cockroach.
They floated the cars across rivers on rafts. They cut the trail with machetes. They winched them up slopes, one tree at a time.
The crossing took them more than four months.
In 1972, a British expedition tried it with two Range Rovers. It was led by an army officer, John Blashford-Snell.
They brought engineers, inflatable boats, and aluminum ladders to bridge the ravines.
The Darién alone took them almost a hundred days.
Both teams made it. And both proved the same thing. You can drag a car through the Darién. You just can't drive one.
## carretera
By then, governments were ready to finish the job.
In 1971, the United States agreed to pay two thirds of the cost of closing the gap. Panama and Colombia would pay the rest.
Bulldozers began to cut east, into the province of Darién.
Then, in 1975, a group of environmental organizations sued the United States government.
Their case was not only about the trees.
It was about cows.
South America had foot-and-mouth disease, a virus that tears through herds of cattle. North and Central America had gotten rid of it.
And the only thing standing between the two was the Darién.
A road would let animals, trucks and meat move north. The US Department of Agriculture feared an outbreak that could devastate American ranchers.
In 1975, a federal judge in Washington ordered the work to stop, because the government had not properly studied what the road would do.
The money froze. The bulldozers left.
On the Panamanian side, the road kept crawling forward, slowly, until it reached Yaviza.
And there, it stopped.
Since then, plan after plan has come and gone. Colombia has wanted the road. Panama, again and again, has said no.
Because over the years, the gap stopped being only a wall against disease.
## frontera
Remember, this is the border between Panama and Colombia.
And for half a century, Colombia was at war.
The FARC guerrillas used the Darién as a rear base. A place to rest, to move weapons, and to hide.
Paramilitary groups came after them. Villages on both sides were caught in the middle.
The same jungle that had stopped the Spanish, the Scots and the bulldozers became a highway for cocaine.
Today, the Colombian side is controlled by the most powerful criminal group in the country. The Clan del Golfo.
And they found a new business crossing the Darién, worth tens of millions of dollars a year.
People.
## migrantes
For most of the last decade, the Darién saw a few thousand migrants a year. Sometimes twenty or thirty thousand.
Then Venezuela collapsed. And the pandemic broke the economies of half the continent.
In 2021, Panama counted about a hundred and thirty-three thousand people coming out of the jungle.
In 2022, almost two hundred and fifty thousand.
In 2023, more than five hundred and twenty thousand. In a single year.
Most of them were Venezuelans. But they came from everywhere. Ecuador, Haiti, Colombia, China, even Afghanistan.
And about one in five was a child.
The journey usually starts in Necoclí, a beach town on the Colombian side of the Gulf of Urabá.
Boats carry them across the gulf, to Acandí or Capurganá. Every step has a price, and every price is paid to someone.
Then the walk begins.
Up mountains of mud. Across rivers that reach the chest. Past camps of plastic sheets, and the belongings of the people who went before.
Most take between three and ten days.
On the way, many are robbed. Many women are sexually assaulted. In 2023 alone, Doctors Without Borders treated hundreds of them.
Nobody knows how many die. The rivers carry bodies away, and the jungle hides the rest.
On the Panamanian side, the first place they reach is an Emberá village called Bajo Chiquito. A few hundred people, who at the peak received more than a thousand every single day.
From there, canoes take them to reception camps run by Panama. And from the camps, buses take them north, toward Costa Rica, Mexico, and the border of the United States.
The gap that no road could cross had become one of the most dangerous migration routes in the world.
## mosca
But there is something else the Darién holds back. And almost nobody knows about it.
A fly.
The New World screwworm. Its larvae burrow into the open wounds of living animals, and eat them from the inside.
For decades, it was a plague for ranchers in North America.
So the United States fought back in the strangest way. It bred flies by the billions, sterilized them with radiation, and dropped them from planes.
The sterile males mated with wild females. The eggs never hatched. And year after year, the fly was pushed south.
Out of the United States. Out of Mexico. Out of Central America.
Until it reached the narrowest place it could be stopped. The Darién.
For years, a plant in Panama has produced sterile flies, to be dropped over the jungle every week. A living wall, made of flies.
In 2023, the wall broke.
The screwworm jumped the barrier and spread north. Panama, Costa Rica, Nicaragua, Honduras. And by the end of 2024, Mexico.
The United States closed its border to Mexican cattle.
Five hundred years after Balboa, the Darién is still the gate between two continents. And it still decides what gets through.
## hoy
In July 2024, Panama got a new president, José Raúl Mulino. He promised to close the Darién.
His government put up barbed wire on some of the trails. And signed a deal with the United States to fly migrants home.
That year, about three hundred thousand people still crossed.
Then, in 2025, the flow collapsed.
With the new American government closing the door at the other end of the route, the reason to walk through the jungle disappeared.
In some months of 2025, fewer than a few hundred people came out of the Darién. And some of the people heading north turned around, and went back south by sea.
The camps emptied. Bajo Chiquito went quiet again.
And the road? It's still not there. The asphalt still ends in Yaviza. And the Pan-American Highway still has exactly one gap.
## cierre
For three million years, this land was a bridge. For animals, for plants, and for the first people who walked into South America.
For five hundred years, it has been a wall. For the Spanish, for Scotland, for the engineers, for the bulldozers, and for a fly.
And for half a million people, in a single year, it was a door.
A road would make the Darién easy. And that is exactly what everyone who protects it is afraid of.
Every road ends somewhere. This one ends where the map says no.
